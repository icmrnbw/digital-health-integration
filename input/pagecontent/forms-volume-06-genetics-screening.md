Volume 6 covers the "mother and child" screening programme: checking an unborn baby for serious conditions during pregnancy, and checking a newborn in its first days of life. It is run in dedicated screening centres and is closely tied to ordinary pregnancy care in [volume 2](forms-volume-02-ambulatory.html) and the birth itself in [volume 1](forms-volume-01-inpatient.html).

### What screening involves

Screening means testing people who seem healthy to catch hidden problems early. This volume has two distinct halves, joined by the birth:

- Before birth (prenatal). During pregnancy, the mother has ultrasound scans and blood tests at set points (the "trimesters"). These do not give a diagnosis; they estimate the chance that the baby has a condition such as Down syndrome. If that chance is high, the mother is offered a more definitive but more invasive test, where a sample is taken from the womb and the baby's chromosomes are examined directly. Because that test carries a small risk to the pregnancy, the mother must give informed consent, and she is free to decline.
- After birth (neonatal). In the first days of life the baby's heel is pricked for a few drops of blood, which are tested for a handful of rare but treatable conditions. If a result is abnormal, the baby is re-tested and investigated further before anything is concluded.

The important idea for a non-expert is that screening is a funnel: many people are screened, a few are flagged, and only those few go on to the definitive tests. The forms follow that funnel.

### How the forms relate

The prenatal track is a decision chain. The exam card (159) opens the file. The trimester ultrasound (162-164) and biochemical screening (156-157) produce a risk estimate. A high risk leads to a counselling step where the mother either consents (152, 153) or refuses (155). Consent unlocks the invasive procedure (166), which produces a cytogenetic result (154), which feeds the genetic conclusion (165, 172) and, in difficult cases, a panel discussion called a perinatal consilium (173). Each step gates the next, so the forms are genuinely sequential.

The neonatal track is a shorter chain: the heel-prick screen (167) leads, only if abnormal, to confirmatory tests (168, 170) and a retest (171).

A third small group is the registry for people already known to have a hereditary disease (158) and their specialist check-ups (160, 161).

### A sample pathway

Imagine a woman early in pregnancy, referred from her polyclinic (037-1) to the screening centre, where an exam card is opened (159). Her first-trimester ultrasound (162) and blood screen (156) come back showing an increased chance of a chromosomal condition. A counsellor explains the options, and she decides to go ahead with the definitive test, signing a consent form (152). The procedure is done (166) and the laboratory examines the baby's chromosomes (154). The result is discussed by a panel (173) and a genetic conclusion is issued (165). Months later, after a healthy birth, the newborn has the routine heel-prick screen (167), which is normal, and no further action is needed.
